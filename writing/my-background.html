MY PROGRAMMING BACKGROUND

October 29, 2026

BEFORE

I was twelve years old, brimming with thoughts, ideas, and questions. Like many twelve-year-old girls with unrestricted curiosity and an internet connection, I spent time online, passively absorbing the chaos of the world; I was enthralled by the internet and all it had to offer. A considerable amount of my free time was spent in front of a screen, exploring whatever happened to reel in my attention that day. The internet felt infinite, and I was more than keen to wander around in it.

I had never stopped to consider how any of it worked. Websites were just places for stopping by and taking a look around. I visited them, clicked around, occasionally became far too invested in something inconsequential, and moved on.

Then, through the rather unceremonious scheduling of a middle-school elective, I found myself enrolled in a class called "Technology." In retrospect, this timing was quite divine. Even with a well-established fascination with the internet, my understanding of it remained limited to what I could see directly in front of me.


"TECHNOLOGY"

Our introduction to web development came courtesy of a brutalist application with all the visual charm of a blank sheet of paper: Windows Notepad. I typed a few lines of HTML, saved the file, and opened it in a browser to discover… I made a webpage.

It was rudimentary and bare bones, but for the first time, I was looking at the internet from the other side. This was the dark side of the moon.

We were granted the freedom to make our webpages about anything that interested us. If memory serves me correctly, my first webpage was about snakes. I assume 12-year-old me had her reasons for that.

I remember being fascinated by the fact that I could put something I cared about onto a page and decide exactly how it would look. The subject didn't need to be particularly impressive or important. It just needed to interest me. For someone who had spent so much time exploring things other people had made, the realization that I could make something myself was gripping.


THE IMPACT

Part of what made HTML and CSS so appealing was the way they reconciled two qualities that initially seem contradictory: structure and freedom.

Growing up, I often felt an expectation to have everything figured out and to do everything correctly. Vulnerability was taboo. I was far more accustomed to meeting expectations than exploring what I wanted for myself. In that class, I was given a set of tools alongside the freedom to make something entirely my own. There was no single correct way for my webpage to look, no predetermined subject I had to write about, and no expectation that my interests needed to serve a greater purpose. Nobody needed a twelve-year-old's website about snakes, but I was allowed to make it anyway.

There was an unexpected amount of vulnerability in that freedom. Making something personal meant revealing a bit of myself through the decisions I made. It required a kind of openness I hadn't practiced before, and it was a bit intimidating.

When you're accustomed to meeting expectations, being asked what you want can feel like a much more difficult question than it should. Making something personal meant making decisions that reflected my own interests, preferences, and imagination. It meant allowing a little bit of myself to exist somewhere outside my own head, available for someone else to see.


METHOD TO THE MADNESS

I've always loved math. I revel in technical, procedural work, and I find a level of satisfaction in following a logical sequence toward a result. Understanding the rules of a system and figuring out how to work within them is rewarding. At the same time, I've always had a creative side. I appreciate having the freedom to experiment, make something my own, and approach a problem from different angles. In practice, I’ve found that programming allows those characteristics to coexist happily.

Programming brings rules that must be followed, syntax that must be understood, and problems that demand patience and precision. Solutions don't always work simply because you want them to work. A misplaced character or a flawed assumption can completely unravel an otherwise reasonable idea that you’ve spent hours on.

Within all those constraints, there's an astonishing amount of room to experiment. A problem might have dozens of possible solutions. Two people can approach the same task, use the same language, and produce entirely different results. There's an element of individuality in the decisions you make: how you structure a solution, what you prioritize, what you simplify, and what you decide is worth the extra effort. It's both methodical and imaginative.

I love the satisfaction of finally understanding something. I love the moment when a stubborn problem gives way to a solution. I love that, after all the planning, debugging, questioning, and occasional staring at the screen in disbelief, you can end up with something tangible that exists because you figured out how to make it work.


NOW

My interests have expanded considerably since that first webpage. I've explored C#, C++, Java, Python, SQL, even machine learning. I've worked on projects that bear very little resemblance to the things I made in that middle-school classroom, although I like to think my twelve-year-old self would be somewhat proud.

Lately, I've become especially interested in the intersection of programming, data, and decision-making. Information is everywhere, but information alone doesn't necessarily tell us much. I find it fascinating how data can be cleaned, organized, analyzed, and transformed into something that helps us understand the world a little better.

There's a creative element to that process that I don't think gets enough credit. Finding patterns, asking the right questions, deciding how to communicate what you've discovered are all choices that require more than technical proficiency.

One of my recent projects, for example, involves analyzing insurance claims data, building predictive models, and creating an interactive dashboard to make the results easier to explore. Yeah, it’s a far cry from a webpage about snakes. Beneath this additional complexity, the appeal remains remarkably familiar. I still enjoy learning how something works, figuring out how the pieces fit together, and watching an idea gradually become something real.

Somewhat fittingly, building this website has brought me back to where it all began. I've created quite a few websites over the years, but this one feels different. It's the most hands-on I've been in a while, and I've found myself getting absorbed in the details and decisions that come with it. There's something wonderfully satisfying about having a space on the internet that I can shape entirely around my own interests and ideas. I suppose that's what I was so excited about at twelve, too.

I'm still learning, and I imagine I always will be. That's one of my favorite things about programming. There's no definitive point at which you can declare that you've figured it all out. There will always be another language to explore, another problem to solve, another concept that challenges something you once thought you understood.

That might sound exhausting to some people, but, personally, I find it comforting knowing there is always room to grow.
